The experimental ideal — and why observational data breaks it
🎯
03 · Foundations
Pasteur's sheep, and the counterfactual you never get to see
▼

Before any of the observational techniques, establish what a perfect randomized experiment looks like. Everything else today is an attempt to approximate this when you can't have it.

Louis Pasteur, 1882

  • 50 sheep, randomly assigned into two pens of 25
  • Treated group vaccinated; control group not
  • All 50 then given a lethal dose of anthrax
  • Every unvaccinated sheep died. Every vaccinated sheep survived

Clean randomization made the causal inference unambiguous. No controls, no diagnostics, no argument required — the design did the work.

Why humans break it

Ethical and practical constraints kill the same approach. You cannot randomly assign people to receive anthrax, to smoke during pregnancy, or to be denied hospital care. The design you'd want is the design you're not allowed to run.

The counterfactual problem

For any individual we only ever observe one potential outcome. The person who went to the hospital and stayed sick — we never see what would have happened had they stayed home. The person who stayed home and recovered — we never see the version who went. Every technique today exists to construct a credible counterfactual for the outcome you didn't get to observe.

The hospital / COVID example

  • What the observational data shows: hospitalized patients stayed sick; non-hospitalized patients recovered
  • The naive conclusion: hospitals are harmful
  • The actual issue: sicker patients self-select into the hospital. The two groups were never comparable to begin with
  • What randomization would fix: it makes the groups statistically equivalent before treatment, so the post-treatment difference is the treatment

The twin analogy

If one twin gets the treatment and the other does not, the untreated twin is the perfect counterfactual — same genes, same upbringing, same everything except the treatment. Randomization is how you approximate that at scale: it makes treated and control groups statistically identical on average.

04 · Foundations
Class size, education and earnings — what OLS with controls can and can't do
▼

The question: do smaller class sizes increase student performance?

The ideal experiment the room designed

  • Random assignment to small / medium / large classes
  • Baseline testing before treatment
  • Same resources at home; teacher quality controlled for
  • Multi-year follow-up, across multiple subject types

Why it doesn't happen: humans have agency — parents can simply refuse the assignment. It's expensive to scale. And there are ethical objections to deliberately putting some children in worse conditions.

So what goes wrong with the observational data

If students in smaller classes perform better, that could reflect wealth, parental education, school quality, or after-school resources — not class size at all. Wealthy families cluster in districts that happen to have smaller classes and more educational investment of every other kind.

The line to keep straight

Strong correlation does not imply causation — but a causal relationship could still be there. The correlation isn't evidence for causality; it also isn't evidence against it. That's the whole reason the rest of the day exists.

Education and earnings — the pre-work dataset

  • Positive but noisy correlation between years of schooling and earnings
  • Curvilinear pattern: some individuals with 10 years of schooling out-earn people with 16–17 years
  • Possible explanations raised: degree type, school prestige, or career choice — someone with many years of schooling may deliberately enter a lower-paying field

OLS with controls

Regressing earnings on education with age, age², Black, Hispanic and married as controls still leaves a positive, statistically significant coefficient on education. Running it again on the PSID as a second data source gives the same positive, significant result after the same demographic controls.

Why age² is in there: earnings may peak and then decline as people shift priorities — his example was the finance career that turns into an antique dealership in Vermont. A linear age term can't bend; age² can.

05 · Method rule
The kitchen-sink problem — why more controls is not better
▼

Throw enough correlated controls into the regression and the education coefficient gets swamped — a real causal effect can be made to look like it disappeared. This happens mechanically, not substantively: other variables correlated with education absorb its explanatory power.

Seamans' rule for including a control

Include a variable only if there is a theoretical story or literature support for its relevance. Not because it lifts fit. If you don't have the domain knowledge yet, a literature review buys it fast — that's the cheap part of the work, and it's what separates a defensible specification from a fishing trip.

The two questions are not the same question

Kitchen sinkCausal specification
Asks"What best predicts earnings?" vs. "Does education cause earnings?"
SelectionEverything correlated with the outcome vs. only what theory or literature justifies
SuccessBest fit vs. a coefficient you can defend as causal

This is exactly where a data scientist's instinct — maximise predictive fit, keep whatever helps — pulls in the wrong direction. It's the right instinct for a different job.

The parental wealth example

Wealthy parents can pay for more education, better schools, tutoring and connections. That may well matter more than education itself. But — and this is the point — education can still have an independent causal effect underneath it. Controlling parental wealth into the model doesn't settle the question; it just moves where the argument has to happen.

Project STAR — a real randomised experiment, and its cracks
🧪
06 · Case
Project STAR — the design, and why there are three arms
▼

Tennessee Student Teacher Achievement Ratio (STAR), late 1980s. Analysed by Alan Krueger, the Princeton labor economist — likely a Nobel candidate, who passed away prematurely.

The design

  • 11,600 students and teachers, randomly assigned to one of three conditions
  • Randomization happened within schools, not across schools — that holds geographic and socioeconomic factors constant, so you're not accidentally comparing a rich district to a poor one
  • Students were intended to stay in the same class type for four years, kindergarten through third grade
ConditionClassWhat it isolates
1 · Small13–17 studentsSmall class and low student–teacher ratio
2 · Regular22–25 studentsBaseline
3 · Regular + aide22–25 students, full-time teacher's aideLow ratio without a small class
Why three conditions and not two

The third arm separates class size from student–teacher ratio. If only class size matters, the aide condition should perform no differently from plain regular. If only the ratio matters, a class of 100 with 10 teachers should perform the same as a class of 10 with 1 teacher. The aide arm is the only thing that can tell those two stories apart — which is the entire reason it's in the design.

📊
07 · Case
Project STAR — Table 1, the main result, and why low R² is fine
▼

Table 1 is the randomization check

Summary statistics exist to answer one question: did the randomization actually work? You compare the groups on things treatment couldn't have caused, and look for balance.

  • Poverty rates, race and age look similar across conditions; joint p-values mostly above 0.05
  • ⚠️ The poverty rate difference approaches significance — kids in small classes were slightly less likely to be classified as poor. That's imperfect randomization, and you have to say so
  • ⚠️ Attrition is statistically significantly higher in larger classes (joint p ≈ 0.02) — families were more likely to pull their children out of the bigger classes

The main finding

  • Students in small classes scored ~4–5 percentile points higher on standardized tests at end of year — statistically significant
  • Regular class with a teacher's aide: positive coefficient but not statistically significant
  • → It's class size that matters, not the student–teacher ratio. That's the payoff of having the third arm
  • Adding school fixed effects and demographic controls does not materially change the result — which is what you want to see

The R² point

R² ranges from 0.01 to 0.31 across specifications. That is fine. The goal here is causal identification, not prediction — you are not trying to explain test scores, you are trying to isolate one coefficient you can defend. A causal paper with an R² of 0.01 can be completely correct.

The non-parametric result

Beyond the regression: the full distribution of third-grade test scores shifted rightward for small-class students versus regular-class students. Not just the mean moving — the whole distribution. That's a much harder result to explain away than a single significant coefficient.

⚠️
08 · Case
Project STAR — the five things that threaten it anyway
▼

Even a real randomised experiment with 11,600 students has cracks. These are the five worth being able to name, because they generalise to any field experiment.

Attrition
Families in larger classes were more likely to withdraw their children. If the families who leave are also the most motivated, you've quietly removed the strongest students from the large-class group — which biases the comparison in a direction you can't sign without knowing who left.
Switching
Some families refused their assigned class and pressured principals to move their child. The result: class sizes in the "small" condition occasionally reached 18–20, and some families assigned to larger classes moved into the aide condition. Assignment stopped being the same thing as treatment.
Hawthorne
Families who know their child is in an experiment change their behaviour. Parents who believed a small class meant their kid was marked as smart may have pushed them harder — so part of the measured effect is the parents, not the class.
Substitution
Families assigned to large classes compensate. More tutoring, more after-school programs. That narrows the measured gap — so the estimate is conservative relative to a world where nobody could compensate, which is not the world policy operates in either.
Scalability
~11,000 students in a few Tennessee areas. Scaling to all of Tennessee — let alone the US — would need hundreds of thousands of additional teachers and bigger school buildings, at enormous cost. An effect that's real at experiment scale can still be unbuildable at policy scale.
The epistemics line

A hardcore labor economist would say the data are "consistent with a causal argument" rather than proof of causality. Randomization here is strong evidence — it is not absolute proof. Use that phrasing; it's the register the field actually speaks in.

Prediction vs. causal inference — two different jobs
🔀
09 · Framing
Nothing plays the role R² plays — the causal work is the argument
▼

Machine learning and causal inference answer fundamentally different questions, and the difference shows up hardest in what you're allowed to use to pick between approaches.

Machine learningCausal inference
QuestionWhat best predicts Y? vs. Does X cause Y?
DiagnosticsR², cross-validation error, AUC vs. approach-specific checks (first-stage F for IV, balance for matching, lambda for Heckman)
Model choicePick the best predictive fit vs. no statistic picks it for you
Insignificant termsDrop them vs. keep them — they can be the finding
What you shipA model vs. a model plus an argument
The thing to actually memorise

Causal inference has diagnostics — the first-stage F-statistic for IV is a real one. But those diagnostics cannot be used to select among approaches the way R² selects among predictive models. You must argue that your approach is appropriate, not just show that a diagnostic passed. There is no number you can point at instead of thinking.

Insignificant ≠ discardable

In predictive work you throw out variables that don't earn their place. In causal work that's a mistake. The null result on the teacher's-aide condition in STAR is itself an important finding — it's what rules out student–teacher ratio as the mechanism. Delete it because p > 0.05 and you've deleted the answer.

Matching — building a counterfactual out of observables
🎚️
10 · Method
Propensity score matching — one scalar, and three knobs
▼

The motivation: when randomization isn't available, construct the counterfactual by matching each treated unit to an observationally similar untreated one.

The intuition

Jerry went to the hospital. Find the untreated person who looks most like Jerry — Rob: similar age, married 20+ years, glasses, facial hair — and compare their outcomes. Rob is standing in for the version of Jerry who stayed home. It's the twin analogy, executed on data instead of genetics.

The propensity score

  1. Run a probit regression of the treatment indicator on the observable covariates
  2. That collapses all the observables into a single scalar between 0 and 1 — the probability of receiving treatment
  3. Match on that scalar, not on the full covariate vector. One number instead of twenty

Common support

The range of propensity scores where both treated and untreated observations exist with meaningful density. If the two populations look very different there's little common support and good matches are hard to find.

  • Dittmar's printing press example: adopters cluster at high propensity scores, non-adopters at low ones; common support exists roughly between 0.15 and 0.8
  • There's some art in defining the support region — no strict rule of thumb. Which means you should show the plot, not just assert the range

The two knobs — caliper and replacement

KnobTighter / offLooser / on
CaliperBetter matches, but more treated observations dropped because no close match existsMore observations retained, but the matches are less similar
ReplacementNo replacement — each control used at most once; some treated units get worse matchesWith replacement — better matches, but some control units carry very high weight and may be outliers

Caliper = the maximum allowable distance between a treated unit's propensity score and its match. Sensitivity analysis is the job: vary the caliper and check whether the result changes materially. If your conclusion only survives at one caliper, you don't have a conclusion.

The critique that never goes away

Propensity score matching only controls for observable characteristics. Unobservable differences — immune system strength, intrinsic motivation — are untouched by it. Current best practice is therefore to use matching alongside another technique (instrumental variables, difference-in-differences) so the result is robust across approaches rather than resting on one set of assumptions.

11 · Cases
Three matching studies — job training, CEO confidence, violent video games
▼

NSW job training — when the naive answer is backwards

National Supported Work Demonstration, mid-1970s: temporary employment, counseling and work experience for disadvantaged workers — high school dropouts, ex-drug addicts, ex-criminals.

  • Naïve OLS: treated workers earned roughly $5,000–6,000 less than untreated workers five years later — i.e. the program looks actively harmful
  • After propensity score matching: the estimated difference falls to about $550 and is no longer statistically significant
  • Sensitivity: tightening the caliper from 0.01 to 0.001 moves the point estimate but not the conclusion — no statistically significant difference in any specification
  • Interpretation: the naive comparison was misleading because the treated were a very different population from the comparison group. Matching didn't find an effect; it removed a fake one

CEO confidence — a capstone group's best-practice example

PwC data, 2021. Question: does CEO confidence predict beating analyst consensus revenue forecasts? Treatment = "very confident" CEOs; control = everyone else.

  • Unadjusted: 41% of "not confident at all" CEOs beat consensus vs. 61% of "very confident" CEOs
  • The worry: reverse causality — CEOs who already have early positive signals are the ones who report high confidence
  • PSM used to match very confident CEOs to similar less-confident CEOs on observables; the common support plot showed substantial overlap
  • ⭐ The key strength: the group varied both caliper size and matching method, and the t-statistic stayed above 2 across every specification
Why he held this up

This is the recommended best practice: show the result does not depend on any single matching choice. Robustness reported up front is what separates a credible matching paper from a lucky one.

Violent video games — the effect that mostly dissolves

6,500 eighth-grade students; heavy violent video game players matched to observationally similar non-players.

Outcome (males)UnmatchedAfter matching
Vandalism~+20%~−4% · not significant
Cheating~+17%Shrinks sharply · not significant
Hit someone~+20%Shrinks sharply · not significant
Marijuana use+6%−3% · not significant
  • Males: the correlation between violent video games and violent behaviour largely disappears once you compare like with like
  • The marijuana flip (+6% → −3%, insignificant) prompted the aside that video games may have a calming effect
  • Females: some effects persist after matching — group fight involvement +12% unmatched → +4% matched, still statistically significant

12 · Cautionary tale
The sustainability paper that only works in one corner of the parameter space
▼

The original

  • 2014, Management Science — top-tier journal, and the most-cited paper in the journal
  • Claim: 180 US companies that voluntarily adopted sustainability policies by 1993 significantly outperformed matched counterparts in stock market and accounting performance over the long term
  • The matched sample showed no statistically significant pre-period differences across observable characteristics — which is exactly what a good matching paper is supposed to show

The replication

  • Andrew King (Boston University) attempted to replicate it and could not obtain the original data
  • He reconstructed the data himself and ran a battery of sensitivity tests — varying caliper, varying matching method
  • Results ranged from strongly positive to negative, intersecting zero in most specifications
  • The original result appears only in a narrow region of the parameter space — which is what cherry-picking a matching specification looks like from the outside
  • The authors have not publicly responded. King's replication paper is not published — it lives on his website
The lesson

With propensity score matching, transparency and robustness testing across many specifications is essential. Reporting only the specification that yields the result you wanted is a serious methodological failure — and the knobs (caliper, matching method, replacement) are exactly the surface where it happens. Report the surface, not the point.

Selection models — when the data itself is missing non-randomly
🪟
13 · Method
Heckman selection — correcting for who ended up in your sample
▼

The motivation: when data are not missing at random, OLS on the observed sample produces biased estimates. Matching fixes who you compare; Heckman fixes who made it into the data at all.

The married-women wage example

  • Dataset of married women — some in the workforce (wages observed), some not (wages missing)
  • The missing wages are not missing randomly: women with more children are less likely to be in the workforce
  • If the women who exit the workforce would have earned lower (or higher) wages, OLS on the working sample gives a biased estimate of the education–earnings relationship

The correction — "Heckit" (Heckman + probit/logit)

  1. Run a selection equation — probit or logit predicting whether the person is in the workforce, using education, age and number of children. Number of children comes back negative and statistically significant: more children → less likely in the workforce
  2. Use the coefficients from that selection equation to construct a correction variable — lambda, the inverse Mills ratio
  3. Include lambda in the main earnings regression, where it absorbs the selection effect
Exclusion restriction — selection-model flavour

Ideally include a variable in the selection equation that predicts participation but does not directly affect the outcome. Here that's number of children: plausibly it moves whether a woman works, but not her wage rate. Same logical shape as an IV's exclusion restriction, same fact that you argue it rather than test it.

What the correction actually did

The education coefficient drops slightly, 0.079 → 0.077. Small, but readable: it suggests women out of the workforce would have earned slightly less than observationally similar working women, so the education–earnings relationship is marginally weaker once selection is accounted for.

Stata has a one-step heckman command; the professor walked through the multi-step version for conceptual clarity, so you can see where lambda comes from rather than trusting a black box.

Bank mergers — Levine et al.

  • Question: does geographic overlap of bank branches affect merger probability, stock returns on announcement, and post-merger performance?
  • Why no randomization: bank CEOs make deliberate, strategic merger decisions. Nobody is randomly assigned a merger
  • Selection variable: distance between the acquirer's state and the target's state — closer means more likely to merge
  • Result: lambda is not statistically significant, which suggests limited selection bias; the coefficients on the overlap variables change little after correction
  • The paper also runs an instrumental variable approach alongside the Heckman correction — the multi-method norm again

Read the insignificant lambda correctly: it isn't a failed correction. It's evidence that selection wasn't driving your result — and it's worth reporting for exactly that reason.

Instrumental variables — borrowing randomness from somewhere else
🔧
14 · Method
2SLS — what an instrument has to do, and what it costs you
▼

The core idea

Find a third variable Z — the instrument — that affects X (the endogenous variable) but does not directly affect Y (the outcome). Z affects Y only through its effect on X: that is the exclusion restriction. This breaks the link between X and the unobserved confounders U, which is what lets you identify the causal effect.

Two-stage least squares

  1. Stage 1: regress X on Z (plus controls) to create X-tilde — a version of X purged of the influence of the unobservables
  2. Stage 2: regress Y on X-tilde (plus controls)

In practice Stata's ivreg runs both stages simultaneously — the two-step framing is conceptual, not something you type twice.

Why standard errors go up — and why that's structural

X-tilde is itself an estimated quantity carrying its own uncertainty. Using an estimated variable as a regressor injects additional noise into the second stage. Standard errors can increase substantially — sometimes the coefficient stays roughly the same size but loses statistical significance. That is IV working as designed, not IV failing.

Diagnostics

CheckRequirement
RelevanceThe coefficient on Z in the first stage must be statistically significant — sign can be positive or negative
StrengthFirst-stage F-statistic must exceed 10. Below that the instrument is weak
ExclusionCannot be tested statistically in the standard case — it must be argued on theoretical grounds

The exclusion restriction is the art component of IV. No diagnostic can confirm it. That's why half of an IV paper is prose about why the instrument is plausible, and why the good instruments in the literature are the ones with a story you can say out loud without flinching.

Vietnam draft lottery — Angrist

  • Between 1970 and 1972, random lottery numbers were assigned to birth dates; men below a cutoff were drafted
  • The number is random by construction → it shouldn't affect earnings except through military service
  • First stage: a low lottery number raises the probability of veteran status by ~16 percentage points
  • Result: military service lowers earnings by roughly $2,000–2,700 per year
  • Imperfections: some draft-eligible men were exempted (health, other reasons); some high-number men volunteered anyway. Not a perfect instrument — but a strong one

Quarter of birth — Angrist and Krueger

  • School entry cutoffs mean children born in Q1 are slightly older when they start school, so they can drop out at 16 with slightly more education than Q4 children
  • First stage: Q1 birth associated with slightly fewer years of education on average — shown graphically across birth cohorts 1930–1940
  • Result: return to education ~0.08 log points per year, similar to the OLS estimates (~0.07–0.079)
  • The critique: strategic parents may plan births to optimise school entry timing, which would correlate birth quarter with parental wealth and hence earnings. He acknowledged you have to work hard to construct that story
  • Standard errors increase relative to OLS, as expected

Printing press and city growth — Dittmar

  • Cities closer to Mainz — where the printing press was invented — were more likely to adopt it earlier
  • Exclusion argument: distance from Mainz shouldn't directly affect a city's economic growth except through press adoption
  • ⭐ The robustness check worth stealing: he reruns it with distance to Amsterdam, London, Paris, Venice and Wittenberg as alternative instruments. None of them matter — only distance to Mainz. That confirms it's the printing press connection driving the result, not mere proximity to a large city
  • The paper also uses propensity score matching in the appendix alongside IV in the main body

Energy drink availability — student capstone

  • Z = distance to the nearest bottling plant. Closer plant → more frequent restocking → higher shelf availability
  • Exclusion argument: consumers do not know or care where the bottling plant is, so distance shouldn't directly affect their purchase decision
  • He noted it's similar in spirit to Dittmar's distance instrument, and praised the approach

Property inspection reports — student capstone

  • Z = inspector leniency. Inspectors are randomly assigned to properties — analogous to the random assignment of patent examiners at the USPTO
  • Some inspectors are historically more lenient, some stricter, conditional on experience and specialty
  • Random assignment means leniency is independent of the property's underlying capex needs; leniency affects capex indirectly through the inspection report but shouldn't directly affect capex risk
  • He highlighted the random assignment feature as the key strength — it's the closest an observational instrument gets to a lottery

Proximity to colleges — Card

  • Z = the number of two- and four-year colleges in the county where the individual grew up. Growing up near colleges may raise educational attainment through exposure and aspiration
  • Caveat he flagged: not a perfect instrument — many other things may differ between areas with more vs. fewer colleges
  • First-stage F = 124, well above the threshold of 10
  • Result: the return to education nearly triples relative to OLS — ~0.048 OLS vs. ~0.13 IV — with correspondingly larger standard errors
  • Stata: ivreg with the endogenous-variable/instrument notation; first-stage results via the first option after the comma

Day 4 exam hooks — the things worth memorising
▼
  1. Every technique today is a counterfactual machine. Pasteur's sheep get one for free; the rest of the day is what you do when you can't randomize.
  2. The hospital example is the selection-bias template. Sicker patients self-select into treatment → the naive comparison says treatment harms. Same shape as NSW job training.
  3. First-stage F must exceed 10, and the first-stage coefficient on Z must be significant. Below 10 = weak instrument.
  4. The exclusion restriction cannot be tested — it is argued. That's the art of IV, and no diagnostic substitutes for it.
  5. IV widens standard errors by construction. X-tilde is estimated, so stage 2 inherits its noise. The coefficient can survive while significance doesn't — that's not a failure.
  6. PSM only balances observables. Unobservables (motivation, immune system) are untouched — which is why best practice pairs it with IV or diff-in-diff.
  7. Caliper and replacement are sensitivity knobs, not settings. Tight caliper = better matches, more dropped units. Replacement = better matches, over-weighted controls.
  8. Reporting one matching specification is the King-vs-sustainability failure. Report the whole surface; the CEO-confidence capstone (t > 2 across every caliper and method) is what good looks like.
  9. Lambda insignificant = limited selection bias. In the bank merger paper that's a reported result, not a dud — and the education coefficient only moved 0.079 → 0.077 in the wage example.
  10. The STAR aide arm is a null that IS the finding. It's what rules out student–teacher ratio and leaves class size standing. Never delete an insignificant coefficient in causal work.
  11. Low R² is fine in causal work — STAR ran 0.01 to 0.31. You're identifying a coefficient, not predicting an outcome.
  12. The kitchen-sink regression answers a different question. "What predicts earnings" ≠ "does education cause earnings". Include a control only with a theoretical story or literature support.
  13. Table 1 is the randomization check, and attrition is what breaks it. In STAR, poverty balance approached significance and attrition was significantly higher in large classes (joint p ≈ 0.02).
  14. Nothing plays the role R² plays. Causal diagnostics exist but cannot select among approaches — you must argue that your approach is appropriate.
  15. Multi-method is the norm, not a flourish. Dittmar runs IV + PSM; Levine runs Heckman + IV. If two different sets of assumptions agree, the result travels.
The one-line summary of Day 4

Every technique today is one answer to the same question: where do I get a credible counterfactual? Randomization builds one, matching finds one in the observables, Heckman corrects for who made it into the sample, and IV borrows randomness from somewhere else. Pick by which counterfactual you can defend — not by which one fits best.

01 · Debrief
Newspaper readability & internet access — the room's verdict
▼

The task: does internet access causally affect newspaper readability? Year 2000 county data, with lightning strikes as the instrument for ISP count.

What every group saw

  • Naive regression: ISP positive and statistically significant across all groups
  • After instrumenting: significance disappears in most years
  • Group 1 ran 2000, 2004 and 2008 — ISP insignificant after IV in all three
  • Group 2 ran year-by-year — 2007 came back borderline, but with a very low coefficient
The room's conclusion

No robust evidence of a causal relationship between internet access and newspaper readability. The positive correlation you get from OLS is confounded — ISPs built out where population and income already were, and those are the same counties whose papers already differed.

The deeper point Seamans was driving at: the same toolkit that establishes causality also establishes its absence. A null from a well-identified IV is a result, not a failed experiment.

What IV is actually for
🧪
03 · Framing
IV is a test of causality, not a repair kit
▼

The single reframe from today: you don't reach for IV only when you already believe there's an effect. You reach for it to find out whether a correlation is causal at all. Three things can happen:

ScenarioWhat it means
Correlation vanishesThe OLS relationship was confounding. Today's exercise, most years.
Sign flipsThe apparent positive is actually negative once unobservables come out. The most counterintuitive and most publishable outcome.
Hidden effect surfacesNo visible correlation in the raw data, but IV reveals a real causal relationship that was being masked.
Carry this into the post-module project

“We instrumented and found nothing” is a legitimate result if and only if your first stage is strong. Otherwise you haven't found nothing — you've found out that your instrument doesn't work.

05 · Practice
IV reporting checklist
▼
  • Always report the first stage. Not optional, not an appendix item.
  • Report the F-statistic. Rule of thumb: F > 10 = strong instrument. Below that, your second stage is unreliable no matter how pretty the p-value looks.
  • More than one instrument → additional tests required. Overidentification tests; details are in the course files.
  • Capstone groups using multiple instruments: he offered Zoom support. That's a free office hour with the person grading you — take it if your group goes multi-instrument.
Why F > 10 and not F > 4

The usual significance bar would be F ≈ 4. The higher bar exists because weak instruments make IV biased toward OLS and blow up standard errors simultaneously — you get a confidently wrong answer rather than an obviously broken one. Your 2003 estimate (SE = 34.0) is the obviously-broken version; the dangerous case is F = 6 with a tight-looking interval.

07 · Method
Fixed effects — the three buckets
▼

Panel data: the same unit observed across multiple time periods. That structure buys you something cross-sections can't offer.

Break unobserved factors into three buckets

BucketExampleFixed by
Varies only at the unit levelA county's culture, geography, baseline institutionsUnit fixed effects — entirely
Varies only at the time levelA recession, a federal law, a national tech shockTime fixed effects — entirely
Varies at both simultaneouslyA county-specific policy change in one yearNothing — this is what's left
The whole logic of the module in one line

Two-way fixed effects clean out buckets one and two entirely. Bucket three is the reason IV, diff-in-diff and RDD still exist. If you can argue your confounder is time-invariant, FE is enough. If it moves with the unit over time, you need an identification strategy on top.

Collinearity note

Stata drops one category (country 1, quarter 1) to avoid collinearity with the constant. Every coefficient is therefore read relative to the dropped group — not in absolute terms. Misreading this is one of the most common errors in a panel write-up.

CommandWhat it does
xi: regress y x i.countryWhat he demonstrated. The xi: prefix is pre-Stata-11 syntax — modern factor-variable notation drops it: just regress y x i.country.
areg y x, absorb(country)Absorbs fixed effects without estimating each coefficient. Much faster on large datasets. Absorbs one dimension only.
areg y x i.year, absorb(country)Two-way FE the built-in way — unit absorbed, year as dummies.

Difference-in-differences — the shape

  • Needs a treatment that hits some units at a known time, plus units it didn't hit
  • Compares the change in treated units to the change in controls — differencing out both fixed unit gaps and common time shocks
  • Core assumption: parallel trends. Absent treatment, both groups would have moved together. Untestable after treatment; you support it by showing pre-treatment trends track
  • The standard visual is the event-study plot — coefficients by period relative to treatment, flat before, jumping after

Regression discontinuity — the shape

  • Needs a running variable with a sharp cutoff that determines treatment (test score ≥ 60, population ≥ 5,000, vote share ≥ 50%)
  • Units just below and just above the cutoff are as good as randomly assigned
  • Estimates a local effect — valid near the cutoff, and you cannot extrapolate far from it
  • Breaks when units can manipulate their position. The standard check is the McCrary density test — look for bunching just past the threshold
  • Sharp RDD = crossing the cutoff fully determines treatment. Fuzzy RDD = crossing only changes the probability, and you instrument treatment with the cutoff indicator — which is IV again

Day 5 exam hooks — the things worth memorising
▼
  1. Naive regression cannot establish causality. IV tests whether a correlation is causal — it isn't only a fix applied once you've decided it is.
  2. IV has three possible outcomes: the correlation vanishes, the sign flips, or a hidden effect surfaces where the raw data showed nothing.
  3. The same tools show absence of causality, not just presence. A clean null is a result.
  4. Always report the first stage, and always report F. F > 10 = strong instrument.
  5. A weak instrument doesn't give you a null — it gives you noise. An IV coefficient of 1.38 with a standard error of 34 is a division by nearly zero, not evidence of no effect.
  6. An instrument can expire. Lightning predicts ISP entry in 2000 (F = 12.5) and nowhere afterwards. Relevance is a property of the period, not the variable.
  7. Significance under one cherry-picked specification is not a finding. Run several, report all, put them in a table with what varies and what results.
  8. “Significant under one or two specs, not under most” is the honest sentence. Say it rather than hide it.
  9. More than one instrument requires additional tests (overidentification). He offered Zoom support for groups that go there.
  10. In a panel, a time-invariant instrument needs a time interaction — slope × year, not slope. Otherwise the unit fixed effects absorb it entirely.
  11. Unobservables split three ways: unit-only, time-only, and unit × time. Two-way fixed effects kill the first two completely. The third is the entire reason the rest of the toolkit exists.
  12. Stata drops one category to avoid collinearity with the constant — every FE coefficient reads relative to the dropped group.
  13. areg absorbs one dimension. For unit and time, use areg y x i.year, absorb(unit) or reghdfe y x, absorb(unit year).
  14. Good instruments are physical or institutional: terrain, rivers, someone else's portfolio rebalancing. Nobody picked them with your outcome in mind — that is the exclusion argument.
  15. Hoxby was challenged by Rothstein over alternative codings of the river data. Even canonical IV papers lose on robustness.

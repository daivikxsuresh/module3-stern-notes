02 · Framing
The Three I's — why good models don't get used
▼

Companies collect enormous amounts of data and use about 3% of it for actual decision-making. Algorithms are intangible and sit in the background — deciding loans, dating matches, what you get recommended. Three barriers explain the gap:

Ignorance
Not knowing what is even possible.
Inertia
Knowing, and resisting the change anyway.
Imagination
Not seeing how a simple model applies creatively.

3M — the inertia story

A Carlson student joined 3M's forecasting division to forecast revenue per business unit off 25 years of history. She tried a range of time-series and deep-learning models, back-tested across the full period, and the winner was an LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory — a sequential deep-learning model). It projected a 6% revenue decline for one business unit next quarter.

Management pushed back: the LSTM is a black box and she couldn't say which factors drove the number. They rejected it and kept the existing process. The actual decline was 5.5%.

The fix

Run a shadow process — model and legacy process in parallel, build trust over time, then switch. Day 3's explainability material exists precisely to avoid this trap.

Patterson Dental — the imagination story

Patterson Dental (Fortune 500 B2B dental supply) copied Amazon and promised next-day delivery to dentist offices. It produced the worst operating-margin quarter in company history.

The mechanism: an office calls in the morning and orders; that gets packed and shipped. The same office calls again that evening and orders more — now requiring expedited shipping. Two shipments, one customer, one day.

The fix was a binary classification model: will this office place a second order today? If yes, hold the morning shipment and consolidate. Every transaction already flowed through SAP, so the data existed. Return was in the millions.

The lesson

The most valuable projects are usually a very simple model plus imagination, not the most sophisticated algorithm.

03 · Foundations
Unsupervised learning — no label, no accuracy
▼

Unsupervised learning has no target variable and no label. The algorithm finds structure on its own. Three practical algorithms in this module:

  1. Association rule mining — market basket analysis
  2. Clustering — customer segmentation
  3. Anomaly detection — finding the unusual
The evaluation problem

With no ground truth there is no objective accuracy metric. Evaluation is judgmental: is the output actionable? The standard validation is to run an A/B test on top of the unsupervised output to confirm the impact is causal.

04 · Association Rules
Support, confidence, and why lift is the one that matters
▼

Association rule mining was the first machine learning algorithm used commercially — Bezos ran it on Amazon transaction data ~30 years ago, before rich customer profiles existed. Amazon started with books: nonperishable, easy to warehouse or drop-ship, and they attract a high-value, data-rich customer. Today ~30% of Amazon sales come from recommendations built this way. "Customers who bought this also bought…" is this algorithm.

The setup

  • Input is a collection of transactions; each is a set of items — the market basket
  • Only presence matters. Quantity and price are ignored
  • A rule is If X then Y: X is the antecedent / left-hand side, Y the consequent / right-hand side
  • X and Y must be non-overlapping item sets

With tens of thousands of SKUs the number of possible subsets runs to the billions — which is why you need an algorithm to find the interesting ones.

The three metrics

MetricDefinitionWhat it's for
ConfidenceP(Y | X) = transactions with X and Y ÷ transactions with X Strength of the rule. Insufficient alone — a popular item lands on the right-hand side of nearly every rule.
Supporttransactions with X and Y ÷ all transactions Prevalence. Filters out rare combinations not worth acting on.
LiftSupport(X∩Y) ÷ [Support(X) × Support(Y)]
= Confidence(X→Y) ÷ P(Y)		 The real signal. How much more often X and Y co-occur than if they were independent.
Reading lift

Lift = 1 → no relationship, exactly what chance predicts. > 1 → positive association. < 1 → negative association. Rule of thumb: look for > 1.5, ideally 2+; 4–5 is excellent.

The general principle

Always compare model output against a sensible baseline. Lift is just the formalisation of "compared to what?" — that idea recurs all module.

Support, made concrete

If 4 transactions out of 1,000,000 contain both crickets and horse meat, confidence might be 80% — but support is 0.000004. Not worth moving a shelf or running a campaign over.

Typical starting values: support 1–2% on large grocery data, confidence 60–80%.

05 · Worked Example
Eight transactions, computed by hand
▼

The lesson of this example is that confidence lies and lift corrects it.

Apple → Beer

  • Confidence = 3/4 = 75% — three of the four apple baskets also contain beer
  • Support(apple) = 4/8 · Support(beer) = 6/8
  • Independent baseline = (4/8) × (6/8) = 3/8
  • Actual joint = 3/8
  • Lift = (3/8) ÷ (3/8) = 1.0
Read that again

75% confidence looks like a strong rule. Lift of exactly 1.0 says there is no relationship at all — beer is simply popular and shows up everywhere. This is the trap.

Rice → Beer

  • Support(rice) = 4/8 · Support(beer) = 6/8 · joint = 4/8
  • Lift = (4/8) ÷ [(4/8) × (6/8)] = 8/6 ≈ 1.33 — above 1, but still weak

Rules are directional

Apple → Beer and Beer → Apple are two different rules:

  • Confidence(Apple → Beer) = 3/4 = 75%
  • Confidence(Beer → Apple) = 3/6 = 50%

The algorithm evaluates both directions independently and filters each against the minimum confidence threshold separately.

06 · Hyperparameters
The apriori property and the practical loop
▼

Minimum support and minimum confidence are hyperparameters — you supply them, they are not estimated from data. There is no universal setting; it is iterative.

Too high
Zero rules returned.
Too low
Billions of rules, all noise.

The apriori property

If an item set has a given support, no superset of it can have higher support. If chocolate appears in 10% of transactions, chocolate + pears cannot exceed 10%. That single fact lets the algorithm prune the search space dramatically before confidence is ever applied — it is why this is tractable at all.

The loop

  1. Set minimum support (e.g. 1%) and confidence (e.g. 75%)
  2. Run apriori() → a filtered set, ideally 20–100 rules
  3. Sort by lift, descending
  4. Inspect the top 5–10 for actionability
Any data type works

Bin numerics into categories (age → 18–25, 25–35, 35–45); one-hot encode categoricals as 0/1. That gets you rules like if male AND age 25–35 AND tennis elbow → hip pain. The algorithm doesn't care what the "items" are.

Beyond retail

  • Product placement — closer together helps time-pressed shoppers (Manhattan); further apart drives impulse buys on the walk (suburban). The data-science answer is an A/B test, not an opinion
  • Health insurance claims — disease codes in a claim are structurally identical to items in a basket; co-occurrence reveals comorbidity. Same algorithm, different domain, only data-engineering imagination required
  • App checkout — Grofers (India) found app baskets smaller than in-store, and used rules to recommend add-ons at checkout

08 · Clustering
k-means, SSE, and what makes a solution good
▼

Group many objects into a few meaningful segments with no predefined labels. You cannot send a million customers a million campaigns; three or four segments let you say distinct things at scale. Coke's arc: one mass ad in the 1980s → segmented campaigns → personalisation.

What "good" means

  • High intra-cluster similarity — members of a cluster resemble each other
  • Low inter-cluster similarity — clusters sit far apart

Measured by Sum of Squared Errors (SSE): for each cluster, sum squared Euclidean distances from every point to its centroid, then add across clusters. Lower SSE = tighter clusters. It's how you compare solutions across different K and different random starts.

Distance metrics

MetricUse
EuclideanThe default. Pythagoras extended to any number of dimensions — identical maths in 2D, 20D or 1,400D.
ManhattanSum of absolute differences — navigating city blocks.
JaccardBinary data — presence/absence.

Pandora encodes each song into 400 musical dimensions (distortion, rhythmic syncopation, dreamy lyrics…) hand-labelled by musicologists. The recommendation is a nearest-neighbour Euclidean calculation in 400-dimensional space — conceptually no harder than two.

The algorithm

  1. Choose K — a hyperparameter, your call
  2. Randomly pick K points as initial centroids ("team captains")
  3. Assign every point to its nearest centroid by Euclidean distance
  4. Recompute each centroid as the mean of its members — it need not be an actual data point
  5. Reassign every point to the nearest updated centroid
  6. Repeat 4–5 until no point changes cluster (convergence)
Always use multiple random starts

Run 10+ and keep the lowest SSE. Different starts converge to different local optima — one start can hand you a materially worse answer with no warning. In R this is nstart = 10.

Choosing K

  • Business constraint — "we have budget for 4 campaigns" → K = 4. Defer to practical reality
  • Elbow curve — plot SSE against K = 1…10 and find the sharpest flattening
  • In practice the elbow is often not sharp; combine the maths with business input

Describing clusters

Add the cluster label back to the un-normalised data, then compute each cluster's centroid — the mean of every feature — and describe it in plain English. Mall of America: cluster 1 = young leisure shoppers, 7 hours in mall, 37 access points; cluster 2 = basement employees (useless for marketing); cluster 3 = theme-park visitors.

The 1% cluster

If a cluster holds ~1% of members, they are probably anomalies rather than a segment. Either reduce K, or hand those rows to anomaly detection. This connects directly to LOF below.

Sweet spot for practical segmentation: 10–15 features — enough to be informative, few enough to stay interpretable.

Airline case

A mid-size Minneapolis airline with no customer insight. Features engineered off the transaction/ERP system: flight frequency, days booked in advance, % round trips, solo vs group. K = 5 gave one-way travellers (22%, 98% one-way trips), solo travellers (89% travel alone), and three more — five distinct messages instead of one generic blast.

09 · Normalization
The step that silently decides your answer
▼
The rule

Whenever you see the word "similar," think normalization. It applies to k-means, k-nearest neighbours, LOF, and every other distance-based method.

Euclidean distance is dominated by whichever variable has the largest scale. Income in dollars (0–200,000) against height in metres (1.5–1.9): income overwhelms it completely and height contributes essentially nothing.

The damning part — change the units (dollars → $100k units, metres → millimetres) and which variable dominates reverses. Same people, opposite clustering. The unit of measurement is not a modelling decision, so it must not drive the result.

MethodFormulaWhen
Min-max(x − min) ÷ (max − min) → [0, 1] Preferred when the data includes binary 0/1 features, so everything shares a scale. Caveat: sensitive to future values beyond the current max.
Z-score(x − mean) ÷ standard deviation Centres at 0 with unit variance. More robust to future extremes, since mean and SD move less on one new outlier.

10 · In R
RFM segmentation, 2,000 customers
▼

Features: monetary value, recency, frequency, tenure. RFM = Recency, Frequency, Monetary — the standard marketing segmentation frame.

  1. Inspect — str() for types and dimensions; skim() for missing counts, completion rate, mean, SD, quartiles and an inline histogram
  2. Missing values — replace numeric NAs with the column median (robust to skew)
  3. Normalize — drop the ID column first (it is not a feature), then min-max column by column. Re-run skim() and confirm every mean sits in [0, 1]
  4. Elbow — loop K = 1…10, kmeans(..., nstart = 10), store $tot.withinss, plot with ggplot2. This dataset elbows at K = 4
  5. Fit — set.seed() then kmeans(data, centers = 4, nstart = 10); append the cluster column to the original un-normalised frame
  6. Centroids — group by cluster, mean each feature in original scale. Cluster 3 = high monetary, high frequency, high recency, long tenure = the segment to defend
  7. Visualise — a parallel coordinates plot shows every dimension at once, one line per cluster. This is the move when you have 20+ dimensions and 2D/3D plots are impossible
Reading skim() output

mean ≈ median → roughly symmetric, mean imputation is fine. mean ≫ median → right-skewed, use median imputation. Wildly different column scales → that is your trigger to normalize.

11 · Anomaly Detection
Local Outlier Factor (LOF)
▼

LOF — Local Outlier Factor. It finds points that are unusual relative to their own local neighbourhood in multi-dimensional space, rather than against a global threshold. A point is an outlier when its neighbourhood is much less dense than its neighbours' neighbourhoods are.

ScoreMeaning
≈ 1As dense as its neighbours — normal
> 1.5Flag as an anomaly (rule of thumb)
higherMore anomalous
  • Key hyperparameter: number of nearest neighbours (K), typically 3–5, odd numbers preferred
  • Always normalize first — it is distance-based
  • In R: lof(data, minPts = 5) from the dbscan package
  • Output is a score for every row

Applications: fraud detection, network intrusion, quality control, people analytics, sensor monitoring.

Call centre case — the nephew and the superstar

Features: average tickets/day, customer rating, tardies, graveyard shifts, weekend shifts, sick days, and % of sick days taken on a Friday. Workflow: load → skim → normalize columns 2–11 → LOF with 5 neighbours → plot the score density → filter LOF > 1.5. Two people came out.

Negative outlier
The manager's nephew. Rating 2.33 vs 3.49 average · 145 tickets/day vs 156 · 3 tardies vs 1.46 · 1 graveyard shift vs 2 · 0 weekend shifts vs ~1 · 6 sick days vs ~2 · 83% of sick days on a Friday vs 35% average. A data-driven case for HR action.
Positive outlier
The superstar. Rating 4.49 vs 3.49 · 165 tickets/day vs 156 · 0% Friday sick days · above-average weekend shifts.
The point of the case

The same algorithm, run once, found both the worst and the best performer in a 10–11 dimensional space — and neither would show up on any single-variable threshold.

Free anomaly detection

A k-means cluster with very few members is already telling you those points are far from everything else. Clustering is a free preliminary anomaly screen before you run LOF at all.

12 · Supervised Learning
Labels, leakage, and imbalance
▼

Now there is a labelled outcome, and the algorithm learns a mapping from inputs to output. The goal is a model that generalises to unseen data, not one that fits the training set.

TypeOutcome
Binary classification0 or 1 — churn, default, malignant/benign
Multi-classOne of several categories — object detection
Numeric predictionA continuous number — spend, revenue, price

Binary classifiers output a probability, and you choose the decision threshold. Default 0.5, but it is a business decision, not a default to accept.

CRISP-DM

Business understanding → Data understanding → Data preparation → Modeling → Evaluation → Deployment.

Modeling and evaluation are deliberately separate steps: the data used to train must differ from the data used to evaluate. And ~70% of project time goes to data engineering and feature creation, not to running algorithms.

Two requirements people get wrong

Both classes
A commodities company supplied 10 years of contract data — wins only. No losses, so no model could be built. They had to spend another year collecting loss data first.
No leakage
Features must exist at prediction time. Screening resumes, you cannot use tardiness, LinkedIn activity or performance reviews — a new applicant has none of those. Only the CV and cover letter submitted at application are valid inputs.

Class imbalance

If 95% of loans never default, a model that always predicts "no default" scores 95% accuracy and is useless. Accuracy alone misleads — you need precision, recall and lift.

  • SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique) — generate synthetic minority-class points to rebalance toward ~50/50
  • Downsampling the majority also works but throws away information; oversampling is generally preferred
  • Synthetic data has a second use: benchmark competing vendor models on data that mimics the real statistics without exposing protected patient or customer records

13 · Train / Validation / Test
Why you lock the test set away on day one
▼

A model evaluated on its own training data always looks better than it is. Overfitting = learning the noise as well as the signal; underfitting = too simple to catch the real pattern.

  1. Test set (holdout) — randomly set aside 20–25% at the very beginning. Lock it away. Do not touch it until the end
  2. Training set — the remaining 75–80%, used to build models
  3. Validation set — carved out of the training set, used for hyperparameter tuning

K-fold cross-validation — the preferred approach

  • Split the training set into K folds (5 or 10)
  • Each iteration holds out one fold for validation and trains on the other K−1
  • Repeat K times — every row validates exactly once
  • Average across folds: far less noisy than a single validation split, and no data is wasted
Time series is the exception

You cannot split randomly — that leaks the future into the past. Use sequential splitting with an expanding window: train on months 1–3 validate on 4; train on 1–4 validate on 5; and so on.

The step people skip

Once the architecture and hyperparameters are chosen, re-fit on the full dataset — training + validation + test — before deploying. The model is already selected; now use every row you have to estimate its parameters as precisely as possible.

Rules of thumb: 75/25 or 80/20 train/test; within training, 70/10 or K-fold.

14 · Evaluation & Roadmap
Lift curves, and the models still to come
▼

Evaluating

  • Accuracy — proportion correct on the test set
  • Confusion matrix — actual vs predicted; exposes false positives and false negatives
  • Lift — sort the test set by predicted probability descending, then compare the conversion rate in the top N% against the random baseline
Why lift is the business metric

Bank loan campaign: no model, a 10% response rate on 50 invitees = 5 conversions. Scoring the top 50 with a decision tree yielded ~43; a neural network ~45. At a $250K lifetime value per mortgage, those two extra conversions are worth ~$500K.

Context decides what "good" means

Predicting whether the S&P 500 rises tomorrow is historically ~50/50. A 55% accurate model would be worth millions at a hedge fund. There is no universal accuracy bar.

The model roadmap

ModelHyperparameterCharacter
Logistic regressionnoneInterpretable. The baseline.
k-nearest neighboursKEuclidean distance — normalize first.
Decision treedepthReads as a flowchart. Fully explainable.
Ensemblestrees, depthRandom forests, boosting — many trees combined.
Neural networkshidden nodes, layersBlack box, high accuracy.

Each more complex model extends the simpler ones — which is exactly why the foundations come first.

Decision tree, worked

2,500 bank customers; features include age, income, years of experience, mortgage, online account, credit-card average spend, CD account. Outcome: accepted the personal loan offer (0/1).

A path to a "1": income < $106K → credit-card average > $3 → CD account > 0.5 → predict 1. Score a new customer by walking them down the branches; the leaf gives the probability — 6 of 7 = 86%.

The trade-off in one line

The tree is fully explainable and any manager can verify the logic. The neural network on the same data lifted top-50 conversions from 43 to 45 — and nobody can read it. That tension is the 3M story again, and Day 3's explainability material is the answer.

Exam hooks — the twelve things worth memorising
▼
  1. Lift = Confidence ÷ P(Y) = Support(X∩Y) ÷ [Support(X)×Support(Y)]. Lift 1 = independence.
  2. Confidence alone is a trap — popular consequents inflate it. Apple→Beer: 75% confidence, lift 1.0.
  3. Rules are directional. Confidence(X→Y) ≠ Confidence(Y→X).
  4. Apriori property — no superset has higher support than its subset. That's the pruning trick.
  5. Support and confidence are hyperparameters; lift is the metric you sort by.
  6. SSE — sum of squared distances to centroids. Lower is tighter. Compares K values and random starts.
  7. Always nstart ≥ 10 — k-means converges to local optima.
  8. "Similar" ⇒ normalize. Min-max for binary-inclusive data; Z-score when future outliers are likely.
  9. Cluster with ~1% of members = anomalies, not a segment.
  10. LOF > 1.5 = anomaly; minPts 3–5; normalize first; catches best and worst.
  11. Unsupervised has no accuracy metric — validate with an A/B test.
  12. Re-fit on the full dataset after model selection, before deployment. Time series never splits randomly.

01 · Framing
When 94% accuracy means you did nothing
▼

The churn dataset is 94% no-churn, 6% churn — the classic imbalanced-class problem. Predict "nobody churns" for every row and you score 94% accuracy having built nothing at all. That is the whole argument against accuracy as a default metric.

Two things to do the moment you see imbalance

  1. Reset expectations. The modelling is harder and raw performance numbers will look worse. That is the data, not your failure.
  2. Switch metrics. Precision, recall and F1 become primary; accuracy becomes a footnote.
Rule of thumb

Any time a class sits under ~20% of the data, start thinking in precision and recall before you fit anything. Below ~5% you also need to fix the training data itself — see SMOTE.

The general principle

Same idea as lift on Day 1: "compared to what?" An accuracy number without its baseline is not a result. The baseline here is the majority-class rule, and it is free.

02 · Metrics
The confusion matrix and its four cells
▼

Layout used in class: rows = what the model predicted, columns = reality. (Conventions differ between textbooks and R packages — always check which way round the table is before reading numbers off it.)

The running example — 100 test rows

CellCountMeaning
TP40Predicted churn, actually churned — caught it
FP10Predicted churn, did not churn — false alarm
FN30Predicted no churn, actually churned — missed it
TN20Predicted no churn, did not churn — correct pass

Accuracy = (TP + TN) ÷ total = (40 + 20) ÷ 100 = 60%. And on the 94/6 churn data, the do-nothing model beats that with 94%. Which is the point.

Reading the table fast

The first letter says whether the model was right (True / False). The second says what the model predicted (Positive / Negative). So a False Negative = the model said negative and was wrong. Decode it that way and you never have to memorise the grid.

03 · Metrics
Precision, recall, F1 — and which one to fight for
▼

Precision — "P lines up with Predictions"

Of everything the model flagged, how much was real?

Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 40 / 50 = 80%

Denominator is the model's own positive calls. High precision with low recall = too conservative — it only speaks when certain, and misses most of the real cases.

Recall (sensitivity) — "R lines up with Reality"

Of everything that actually was positive, how much did the model catch?

Recall = TP / (TP + FN) = 40 / 70 = 57%

Denominator is reality. High recall with low precision = too many false alarms — it flags everything, so being flagged means little.

F1 — the harmonic mean

F1 = 2 × (P × R) / (P + R) = 2 × (0.80 × 0.571) / (0.80 + 0.571) = 66.7%

The harmonic mean skews toward the lower of the two, so you cannot buy a good F1 by maxing one metric and abandoning the other. That is exactly what it is for.

Shortcut worth knowing

F1 also equals 2·TP / (2·TP + FP + FN) — here 80 / 120 = 66.7%. Same answer, no decimals, and it makes the structure obvious: TN never appears in F1 at all. That is why F1 survives class imbalance — the giant true-negative block cannot inflate it.

The trap Bapna called out

A model with precision = 100% and recall = 1/51 is useless. It found one case, was right about it, and missed fifty. Perfect precision is trivially achievable by predicting positive exactly once — which is why precision is never reported alone.

Which to prioritise

Ask what the expensive error is. In cancer, fraud and churn, a false negative — the missed case — is the costly one, so skew toward recall. When acting on a flag is expensive and the miss is cheap, skew toward precision. When you genuinely don't know: F1.

04 · Metrics
Threshold tuning, the ROC curve, and AUC
▼

0.5 is not sacred

Classifiers output a probability; the cutoff that turns it into a class is a choice you make. The default of 0.5 has no special status.

  • Raise the threshold → fewer positive calls → precision up, recall down
  • Lower the threshold → more positive calls → recall up, precision down

Every cutoff produces a different confusion matrix and different metrics from the same model. In the class example a cutoff of 0.8 gave 19/24 correct; 0.5 gave 21/24. The practical move is to sweep every cutoff and keep the one that optimises your chosen metric.

ROC curve

  • y-axis = true positive rate (recall) · x-axis = false positive rate
  • Each point on the curve is one cutoff value
  • Better models bow toward the top-left corner — high catch rate, few false alarms
  • R and Python compute it automatically; you never iterate by hand

AUC — area under that curve

One number summarising performance across all possible cutoffs, which is why it is the metric of choice when the class balance is skewed or the cost structure is still unknown.

AUCVerdict
< 0.70Not useful
0.70 – 0.80Acceptable
0.80 – 0.90Good
> 0.90Excellent
Why AUC is threshold-free

Precision, recall and F1 all describe one operating point. AUC describes the whole ranking. So AUC answers "is this model any good?" while precision/recall answer "is this setting any good?" You need both, and in that order.

📈
05 · Metrics
The lift curve — the one executives actually read
▼

The business-facing complement to ROC. x-axis = share of the population you target, y-axis = lift over targeting at random.

Lift = model response rate ÷ baseline response rate

Universal Bank: the top 50 prospects returned 46 hits versus ~5 expected from a random 50 — roughly 9× lift.

The insight worth keeping

Global accuracy can be mediocre while lift in the top decile is excellent. If you only have budget to call 50 people, you do not care how the model ranks person 400 — you care that the top 50 are dense with responders. Don't kill a model on overall accuracy without checking lift at your actual N.

Why this matters for the contest

The prediction contest is scored on profit generated, not accuracy. That makes lift and the cost-benefit matrix the operative tools — not the metric leaderboard.

TP+$10,000Acquired the customer
TN+$10Saved the cost of annoying someone
FP−$800Targeted someone who was never going to respond
FN−$8,000Missed a customer who would have said yes

Expected profit = (TP × 10,000) + (TN × 10) + (FP × −800) + (FN × −8,000)

What this quietly does

The profit matrix sets your threshold for you. A false negative here costs 10× a false positive, so the profit-maximising cutoff sits well below 0.5 — the maths tells you to over-flag. You never have to argue about precision-versus-recall again; the dollars settle it.

In the Universal Bank run the decision tree won on both F1 and expected profit — but that agreement is not guaranteed. When the metric and the money disagree, the money is the answer.

07 · Case
Cancer detection — 569 biopsies, six models
▼

Dataset: 569 patients, 30 numeric biopsy measurements, two classes (malignant = 1, benign = 0). Split 62% benign / 38% malignant — not severely imbalanced, so accuracy is actually usable here.

The agent-run pipeline (daik.bot / Mentorika), observed end to end

  1. Loaded the CSV, dropped the ID column, encoded M = 1 / B = 0
  2. 75/25 train-test split with feature scaling (normalisation)
  3. Fit six models: logistic regression, decision tree, KNN, random forest, gradient boosting, MLP
  4. Computed accuracy, precision, recall, F1, AUC and a confusion matrix for each
  5. Plotted ROC curves across models

Results

Random forest won on accuracy. Its confusion matrix: 90 TN · 0 FP · 4 FN · 49 TP (143 rows — the 25% holdout of 569).

Why the winner was still the wrong pick

Those 4 false negatives are malignant tumours called benign — the clinically dangerous error. Zero false positives looks impressive and is worth almost nothing here: a false positive costs a follow-up scan, a false negative costs a life. Trade false positives for false negatives all day in this domain.

The neural network had the lowest false-negative count at 3, with 96.5% accuracy and AUC 0.992 (KNN was essentially tied at 0.991).

The decision rule

When two models have similar accuracy, pick the one with fewer false negatives — in a domain where the false negative is the expensive error. That is a domain judgement, not a statistical one, and it is the part the leaderboard will never tell you.

⭐ The granularity point — the real lesson of this case

The tree produces only 7–8 distinct leaf probabilities, with 35 people tied at probability 1.0. Logistic regression produces 499 distinct scores. 09 · Framing
The no-supermodel theorem
▼

There is no single best model. Different algorithms make different assumptions and attack the data differently, so which one wins is an empirical question every single time.

AlgorithmHow it actually works
Decision treeRecursive entropy-minimising splits, column by column
LogisticFits a sigmoid curve to estimate class probability
KNNFinds the k most similar training rows, takes a majority vote

The standard toolkit — run these, in this order

  1. KNN
  2. Logistic regression
  3. Decision tree
  4. Random forest
  5. Gradient boosted machine (XGBoost)
  6. Neural network
Tie-breakers, in order

① If two models are close on your metric, prefer the more explainable one. Logistic regression weights can be read aloud to a business stakeholder; random forests and neural nets cannot. ② If the domain has an expensive error, break the tie on that error (see the cancer case).

The reproducibility warning

Different train/test splits can change which model wins. So a team has to agree on a fixed process and stick to it — same seed, same split, same metric — or you are just re-rolling dice and calling the winner science.

Choosing the metric

  • Imbalanced classes → F1, or precision/recall directly
  • False negatives expensive (cancer, fraud, churn) → skew to recall
  • Unsure → F1 is the safe default
  • You have dollar values → profit/cost matrix, and ignore the rest

10 · Data Prep
SMOTE — manufacturing the minority class
▼

When the positive class is genuinely rare — 4–5% — models stop trying. On the donors dataset (~90,569 non-donors vs ~4,800 donors, ~5% positive) plain logistic regression predicted zero positives; the confusion matrix was useless.

⛔ The one rule that must never be broken

You can do anything you like to the training data. The test data must never be touched. Rebalance, synthesise, resample, impute — all fine on train. Touch the test set and your evaluation is measuring a world that does not exist.

SMOTE — Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique

  • Generates synthetic minority-class points in the distributional neighbourhood of existing ones
  • Goal: rebalance the training set toward 50/50 so the model can actually learn the minority pattern
  • Applied only to training data, before fitting — the test set keeps its true 5% distribution
  • Workflow: fit on SMOTE-augmented train → evaluate on the original, untouched test set

Visual intuition: the rare class is a sparse scatter of red points; SMOTE fills in nearby synthetic red points until the two classes are balanced.

After SMOTE, re-run the whole standard toolkit and compare against the pre-SMOTE baseline to confirm you actually gained something.

11 · Data Prep
Missing values, done properly
▼

The donors dataset is the first genuinely messy data in the course.

ColumnMissing
Income rating~21,000 rows
Wealth rating~44,000 rows
Is homeowner43,000 of 90,000

Dropping every row with any missing value takes 95,000 rows down to ~10,000. Unacceptable — and worse, the survivors are almost certainly not a random sample.

Numeric columns — three tiers

  1. Basic: fill with the column median. Median over mean — robust to outliers.
  2. Intermediate: group first (e.g. by age + education bucket), then impute that group's median.
  3. Advanced: treat the missing column as its own prediction target — run a full train/test pipeline to predict it, fill in the predictions, then use the completed column in the main model.

Categorical columns

Don't use the mode

Filling every missing state with "California" is too crude. Instead create a new factor level called "Unknown". It preserves every row, and it lets the model learn whether being unknown is itself predictive — which it very often is. It also sidesteps the rare-level problem, where a state like Rhode Island appears in test but never in training.

12 · Methods
Neural networks and backpropagation
▼

Architecture

  • Input layer — one node per feature (30 nodes for the cancer data)
  • Hidden layer(s) — each node takes a weighted sum of its inputs and applies a non-linear transformation (sigmoid/logistic)
  • Output layer — a probability for classification, a number for regression
  • Weights live on every connection. They are the entire thing the model learns.

Forward pass

For each row: multiply each input by its weight, sum into the hidden node, apply the sigmoid, pass forward, repeat to the output. With random initial weights the first predictions are garbage — that is expected and it is the starting point.

Backpropagation — the training loop

  1. Compare prediction to the actual label → compute error
  2. Use partial derivatives to work out which direction each weight should move to reduce that error
  3. Nudge every weight in that direction (gradient descent)
  4. Repeat across every row, and iterate — often ~10,000 passes — until the weights converge

That loop is the core of every neural network, from this one to the large language models.

Hyperparameters

  • Hidden layers and nodes per layer — try several configurations (e.g. 5 vs 7 nodes)
  • Decay — a regularisation term penalising large weights, to prevent overfitting
  • Stopping criterion — halt when the error improvement per iteration drops below ~1%

Cancer result

Final: 7 hidden nodes, decay = 0.1. Parameter count = (30 × 7) + 7 biases + 7 output weights + 1 bias = ~225 weights. Accuracy 96.5%, AUC 0.992, and 3 false negatives — the lowest of any model tested. The visualisation shades each line by weight magnitude; all 210 input-to-hidden connections exist.

Trade-offs

Pros: very high accuracy, captures complex non-linear relationships, no distributional assumptions. Cons: a black box you cannot explain to a judge or an executive, no built-in variable selection, computationally expensive, and it overfits readily without careful validation.

13 · Methods
Ensembles — bagging, boosting, stacking
▼

Why they work

Individual models make different kinds of errors. Combine models that are genuinely diverse and the errors partially cancel. Models at ~70% individual accuracy can combine to ~83%.

Diversity is the load-bearing condition — different algorithms, different data samples, or different feature subsets. Five copies of the same model average to exactly that model.

The famous case: the Netflix Prize, where no single model won — a combination of four did.

Majority vote (the simple version)

Fit K diverse models on the same training data; for each test row take the majority class. For regression, average instead of vote.

Bagging — bootstrap aggregating (→ Random Forest)

  1. Randomly sample rows with replacement (e.g. 500 of 1,000) — bootstrapping
  2. Randomly sample features (e.g. 10 of 30 columns)
  3. Fit a decision tree on that bootstrapped subset
  4. Repeat 1,000 times
  5. Final prediction = majority vote of all 1,000 trees

Sampling with replacement means one row can appear several times in a sample, effectively zooming in on regions of the data. Net effect: variance down, bias roughly unchanged.

Boosting (XGBoost)

  1. Fit a simple tree — a weak learner
  2. Compute the residuals (what it got wrong)
  3. Fit a second tree to those residuals
  4. Combine, recompute residuals, fit a third tree to those
  5. Repeat until performance converges

Each tree corrects the mistakes of all the trees before it. Hyperparameters: learning rate, max depth, number of trees, subsampling ratio — caret's train() picks sane defaults and can tune by cross-validation.

Toy example: hours studied vs pass/fail. Tree 1 predicts 0.5 for everyone. Tree 2 finds the split at 2 hours — overestimating above, underestimating below. Adjust by the average residual in each leaf, iterate.

Bagging vs boosting in one line

Bagging builds 1,000 trees in parallel and votes — it attacks variance. Boosting builds trees in sequence, each fixing the last — it attacks bias. That is why random forest is hard to overfit and XGBoost needs its learning rate watched.

Stacking

Train several base models, then feed their predictions as features into a meta-model that learns the optimal way to combine them. More sophisticated than a vote; less common in practice.

Trade-offs

Pros: consistently higher accuracy; random forest in particular is robust to overfitting. Cons: black box, hard to explain, higher compute cost, and genuinely hard to debug.

Numeric prediction — the error metrics
▼

The outcome is a continuous number, not a class label. Note the vocabulary: in Shmueli and in Bapna's usage, "prediction" means a numeric outcome and "classification" means a categorical one.

Where it shows up (from the class brainstorm)

  • Catalog spend — how much will this customer spend
  • Office market rent over time · IT infrastructure and server demand
  • Health insurance premium amounts · machine failure timing
  • Startup valuation from pitch data · semiconductor vendor cost savings
  • Four-year graduation rates

The four error metrics

MetricFormulaWhat it tells you
MEmean(actual − predicted)Bias direction — are you systematically over or under? Weakness: positives and negatives cancel, so it can read zero on a terrible model.
MAEmean(|actual − predicted|)Average error magnitude, in the original units. Forgiving of outliers.
MAPEmean(|actual − predicted| / actual)Error relative to scale. Missing by $2,000 on a $13,000 car is far worse than on a $100,000 car.
RMSEsqrt(mean((actual − predicted)²))The standard. Squaring punishes big misses disproportionately. Reported in the original units.
Why RMSE ≠ MAE — the example

Model 1 misses by exactly 1 on every row → RMSE = 1. Model 2 is perfect except one error of 5 → RMSE = 2.23. Same or lower total error, much worse RMSE. Prefer Model 1 — and if you would rather have the occasional big miss, you should be optimising MAE instead. Pick the metric that matches which mistake actually hurts you.

Every classifier has a regression twin

ModelWhat changes
KNNPredict the average Y of the k neighbours instead of a majority vote
TreeLeaf predicts the average Y of its training rows instead of the majority class
RegressionOrdinary least squares — minimise the sum of squared residuals
Neural netSame architecture, continuous output node
XGBoostSame boosting; the residuals are just numeric errors

Vote → average. That is the whole difference.

15 · Numeric Prediction
Regularisation — ridge, lasso, elastic net
▼

The problem it solves

In wide datasets many columns are noise. Ordinary least squares keeps every variable you hand it, useful or not — more junk features, more noise, worse out-of-sample prediction. Decision trees do feature selection automatically; linear regression does not, unless you make it.

The core idea

Add a penalty term to the objective. A feature now has to pay to stay in the model — its predictive value must exceed its cost.

minimise: sum of squared errors + λ × (penalty on the weights)

λ (lambda) is a hyperparameter — higher λ, stronger penalty, more features squeezed out. Chosen by cross-validation: try ~100 values, keep the one with the lowest validation error.

The three flavours

FlavourPenaltyBehaviour
Ridge (L2)λ × Σ(wᵢ²)Shrinks all coefficients toward zero but never exactly to zero — nothing is eliminated. Good when most features are somewhat useful.
Lasso (L1)λ × Σ|wᵢ|Can zero coefficients out entirely → automatic feature elimination. The default when you suspect junk.
Elastic netλ × [α·L1 + (1−α)·L2]Hybrid. α = 0 is pure ridge, α = 1 is pure lasso. Try several α, keep the lowest validation RMSE.

In the catalog case lasso automatically dropped Source_D, Source_P and other junk catalog columns, and won at RMSE 102.74. Elastic net at α = 0.8 came in at 102.77 — effectively tied.

Practical guidance

Always run lasso alongside standard regression. The extra work is a few lines and the benefit is real. Ridge when you believe most features matter; lasso when you suspect many don't and want the pruning done for you. And any time you run logistic regression, also run logistic with lasso.

The unifying idea

Trees get feature selection for free by construction. Regularisation is how you buy the same property for a linear model — and it is why the penalised linear models beat both the tree and the neural net on catalog spend.

16 · Personalization
Matrix factorisation — recommendations without features
▼

The problem

Standard regression predicts the average user's behaviour. Personalisation needs individual-level predictions: what will this specific person like? Netflix, Amazon, Spotify, OkCupid, LinkedIn.

The data structure — three columns, that's all

Entity A (user/sender) | Entity B (item/receiver) | engagement score (rating, time, clicks)

Bitcoin OTC example: sender ID | receiver ID | trust score (−10 to +10). The matrix is extremely sparse — 6,000 senders × 6,500 receivers = 39 million possible pairs, only ~35,000 observed. That is under 0.1% filled.

What makes this different

There are no features about anyone. No demographics, no genre tags, no product attributes — only the interaction history. The method extracts structure from who interacted with whom, nothing else.

Latent factors — the intuition

  • Assume K imaginary dimensions that both users and items have weights on
  • For movies these might loosely resemble genre, pacing, star power — but they are never named and the algorithm does not know what they mean
  • Each user gets a vector of K weights; each item gets a vector of K weights
  • Predicted rating = dot product = u₁·i₁ + u₂·i₂ + u₃·i₃

Training

  1. Split the observed interactions 75/25
  2. Find the user and item weights that minimise prediction error on training
  3. K is a hyperparameter — try K = 1, 2, 3… and select on validation RMSE
  4. Iterate until the weights converge — same spirit as neural network training

Results

Sender 1 × Receiver 15: predicted 0.57, actual 1 — rounds correctly. Sender 2 × Receiver 20: predicted 6.1, actual 5. Test RMSE 2.84 on a −10 to +10 scale — strong.

Where else it applies (class brainstorm)

DomainA | B | score
LinkedInperson | person | messages exchanged → recommend connections
Job matchingcandidate | job | tenure achieved → recommend jobs
Healthcarepatient | clinic or nurse type | visit frequency → recommend care pathways
Real estateinvestor | property type | historical return → recommend targets
Vendor selectioncompany | vendor | cost savings achieved → recommend vendors
The constraint — and the one to remember

Both of the first two columns must be entities with IDs, and the third must be a numeric engagement proxy. If you have that shape, you can build a recommender with zero features about either side. The vendor row above is the one worth keeping in your back pocket.

17 · Text
Text analytics and the fake review problem
▼

The challenge

Unstructured text cannot be fed to a model — it has to become numbers. A corpus of 1,600 reviews at ~50 words each yields thousands of candidate features, and adding bigrams ("customer service") expands it further.

Pre-processing pipeline

  1. Remove stop words — "a", "the", "but", "from" carry no signal
  2. Lowercase everything — "Customer" and "customer" must be one feature
  3. Stemming — fishing/fishes/fish → fish; conditional/conditionally → condit
  4. Drop very high-frequency words — in nearly every document, so not discriminating
  5. Drop very low-frequency words — in one or two documents, too sparse to learn from

Text → numbers

EncodingValue in each cell
Bag of words1 / 0 — is the word present. Simplest; ignores frequency and context.
Term frequencyCount of appearances in that document.
TF-IDFTF × log(N / df) — penalises words appearing in almost every document. The standard choice.

The fake review dataset

  • 1,600 Chicago hotel reviews: 800 genuine (validated by the hotels) + 800 deceptive (written by paid Amazon Mechanical Turk workers)
  • Split 1,200 train / 400 test
  • Human benchmark: 61.9% — the best a person managed on the test set

The headline

A bag of words and an off-the-shelf model beat humans by 20 points at spotting fabricated reviews. No semantics, no understanding — just which words co-occur. Worth remembering before assuming a task needs a language model.

Explainability from an ensemble

Random forest tracks which words drove the most splits across all 1,000 trees and outputs ranked importance scores — so you get some explainability even out of a black box.

Where labels come from

  • Users label implicitly — the spam button, star ratings, watch time
  • Domain experts label a sample; the model generalises to the rest
  • Crowdsourcing (Mechanical Turk) for scale

Once labels exist, the entire classification playbook applies unchanged: split, fit the toolkit, compare on F1.

Common applications

Spam detection (Gmail's filter was Google's first ML application) · sentiment analysis · fake review and misinformation detection · document categorisation · contract and RFP analysis (comparing statement-of-work language over time) · real estate risk reports from PDF appraisals · earnings-call tone analysis.

R vs. the agents — why you still learn the code
▼

The question Bapna answered head-on

Agents — Claude Code, Mentorika, ChatGPT — can generate and execute an entire modelling pipeline from one prompt. So why learn the steps?

The answer, verbatim in spirit

Agents make "spectacular mistakes that look correct." Without knowing the micro-steps you cannot catch them. Knowing the pipeline is what lets you evaluate any agent's output rigorously. Recommended workflow: use the agent to move fast, then validate against your own understanding.

The Claude Code demo (Universal Bank)

  1. Case PDF + Excel file dropped into a folder; folder set as the working directory
  2. Prompt: "examine the two files and tell me what they are about" → it read both sheets
  3. Follow-up: "build predictive models and score the target list to find the top 50 people to invite"
  4. It chose logistic regression, citing granularity of the probability scores — the same reason the R analysis landed there
  5. Produced a ranked list of all 500 prospects, conceptually matching the R output

Stated limitation: the chat products execute Python, not R — they generate R code for you to run locally.

Reference
Day 2 exam hooks — the fifteen things worth memorising
▼
  1. Accuracy lies under imbalance. 94/6 churn → predict-nothing scores 94%. Under ~20% minority, go straight to precision/recall.
  2. Precision = TP/(TP+FP) — lines up with Predictions. Recall = TP/(TP+FN) — lines up with Reality.
  3. F1 = 2PR/(P+R) = 2TP/(2TP+FP+FN). Harmonic mean, skews to the lower one. TN never appears — that's why it survives imbalance.
  4. Cancer / fraud / churn → optimise recall. The false negative is the expensive error.
  5. 0.5 is not special. Every cutoff gives a different confusion matrix from the same model. Sweep them.
  6. ROC = TPR vs FPR across all cutoffs; each point is one cutoff; better models bow to the top-left.
  7. AUC bands: <0.7 useless · 0.7–0.8 acceptable · 0.8–0.9 good · >0.9 excellent. AUC is threshold-free; precision/recall are not.
  8. Lift = model response rate ÷ baseline. Poor global accuracy can still mean excellent top-decile lift — check lift at your target N.
  9. Profit matrix beats every proxy metric when you have dollars. It also sets your threshold for you.
  10. No supermodel theorem. Run the toolkit in order: KNN → logistic → tree → random forest → XGBoost → neural net. Ties go to the explainable model.
  11. ⛔ SMOTE the training data only. Never touch the test set. Rebalance train toward 50/50, evaluate on the true distribution.
  12. Missing values: numeric → median (not mean), better: group median, best: predict it. Categorical → a new "Unknown" level, never the mode.
  13. Bagging = parallel trees + vote (kills variance). Boosting = sequential trees on residuals (kills bias). Ensembles need diversity or they do nothing.
  14. RMSE punishes big misses (squared); MAE doesn't; MAPE scales by magnitude; ME only shows bias direction and cancels out.
  15. Lasso (L1) zeroes coefficients out; ridge (L2) only shrinks them. Lasso won catalog spend at RMSE 102.74 — beating the neural net. Complex ≠ better.
The one-line summary of Day 2

The metric is the decision. Every choice today — accuracy vs F1, threshold, ROC vs lift, profit matrix — was really the same question: what does being wrong cost, and in which direction? Answer that and the model selection follows.
